## Transcript

**Q1 — Trigger.** *"In a real engagement, what's the actual moment you sit down and start writing the positioning brief? Not what's supposed to happen — what happens."*

> "I start the week after the kickoff workshop closes, but only once the audience research synthesis lands on my desk. If the synthesis isn't there, I don't start. Usually it's week 2 of the engagement. Sometimes week 3 if the client took longer to make their customers available for interviews."

**Q2 — Quality gates.** *"What has to be true about the inputs for it to be worth starting? When do you delay?"*

> "Three things. First, the audience-research synthesis needs at least three customer interviews and one stakeholder interview, with verbatim quotes — not summaries. If we only got two interviews and the strategist tries to extrapolate, I delay and ask for a re-run. Second, the competitive audit has to name at least five competitors with their *own* positioning copy, verbatim, not our paraphrase of what they say. Third, the kickoff scope-agreement has to actually name the audience the client wants to reach. If they wrote 'everybody' or 'the market' or some other ambiguity, I escalate to Iris before I start. Writing positioning to 'everybody' is a guaranteed fail."

**Q3 — Decision criteria.** *"As you write, when you have multiple options, how do you pick?"*

> "First rule: pick the frame that creates the narrowest audience for which the client is unambiguously the right answer. Wider is not better. Second rule: if two frames are plausible, the one with more cited proof points wins. I'm not picking on aesthetics — I'm picking on what survives a sceptical client review meeting. Third rule: I refuse any frame that requires the client to credibly claim something they don't actually do today. Aspirational is fine; non-credible isn't. Fourth: on enterprise engagements, the founder reviews before it goes out. On mid-market, the founder is informed but the review is optional."

**Q4 — Output format.** *"What does the document look like when it's done?"*

> "Eight to twelve pages. Five sections: audience definition (one paragraph plus named demographics or firmographics), the proposition (one sentence plus three proof points each cited), the against-whom (named competitors with what they say and what we say differently), the why-now (a cited market or behavioural shift), and the implication for identity — what tone, what vocabulary, what visual register Lena should pick up. The implication-for-identity section is the handoff to design; if I write it badly, Lena can't design."

**Q5 — Fallback when an input is missing.** *"What do you do when the audience research isn't there?"*

> "If the synthesis is thin and the client refuses to schedule more interviews, I write the positioning anyway, but I label it explicitly as conditional. The brief itself says 'this is the best inference from limited testimony — validate by month 6 with quarterly customer interviews and revisit if invalidated.' I name the conditional in the brief, and I tell Lena to expect a possible revision. I don't pretend confidence I don't have."

**Q6 — Two competing frames.** *"What if two positioning frames both pass your criteria?"*

> "Then I bring both to Iris. The founder picks. I don't put it to a client vote — that's the wrong shape of decision."

**Q7 — Handoff.** *"After you finish, what happens?"*

> "I send it to Lena. She has 48 hours to review. The brief is not handed to identity-system-build until Lena signs off in writing — an email or a Slack thread, archived under the engagement folder. On enterprise engagements, after Lena, the founder also signs off before identity work starts."

**Q8 — When the handoff stalls.** *"What if Lena flags an issue?"*

> "I rewrite the relevant section within two working days. We allow up to two cycles of back-and-forth. Third cycle escalates to the founder — at that point either I'm not getting the brief right or Lena is asking for something the brief shouldn't carry, and the founder decides which."
