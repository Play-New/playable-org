# Interview with Tomás Reis · kickoff-workshop activity

## Frame

The agent explained the purpose: testimony saved verbatim, cited from the activity node. Tomás confirmed.

## Transcript

**Q1 — Trigger.** *"When does the kickoff workshop actually happen?"*

> "Within five working days of contract signature. I schedule it the moment the contract comes back signed. The block is four hours. I pull from the calendars of the four discipline leads — Marco for strategy, Lena for design, Dev for digital product, and me for client services."

**Q2 — Quality gates.** *"What has to be true for the workshop to even start?"*

> "Three things. First, all four leads present for the full four hours. If any of the four can't make any of the slots in the next five days, I escalate to Iris before booking — running short is worse than running late. Second, the client side has named a single point of contact: one human, one email, one phone number, with the authority to approve scope and calendar. If they offer 'a team' or 'a few of us', I push back and wait for one name. Third, on enterprise engagements, the founder also attends — I confirm Iris's calendar before booking."

**Q3 — Decision criteria.** *"During the workshop, what's binding for the studio's pricing? What's negotiable later?"*

> "The scope agreement reached at the workshop is binding on the price. If the client introduces work outside that scope at week 2 or later, that's a scope-change conversation — escalates to the founder per charter §12. We don't quietly expand. The calendar cadence is non-negotiable: weekly check-in at a fixed day and time, agreed in the workshop. Reschedules are allowed; deletions aren't. And the SPOC discipline is hard: the client side names *one* human. If they want 'the marketing team' to be the contact, I decline. Multiple contacts on the client side mean nobody's accountable for scope drift."

**Q4 — Output format.** *"What's the document that comes out?"*

> "Three to four pages. Five sections: named deliverables for the engagement (positioning, identity system, brand book, UI screens, design system documentation, asset handover — checked off per engagement), the calendar week by week with who's responsible per week, the SPOC on both sides, the fixed-price quote tied to the named deliverables, and the change-of-scope clause referencing charter §12. We've run 14 of these in 2025; same template every time."

**Q5 — Fallback when client won't name a SPOC.** *"What do you do?"*

> "Escalate to the founder. The founder either negotiates the SPOC into existence — that's the typical case — or declines the engagement, which has happened twice in 2024-2025. The studio doesn't start work without a named SPOC. We've learned the hard way that the cost of running an engagement with diffuse client-side accountability is higher than the cost of a lost engagement."

**Q6 — Fallback when one lead can't make it.** *"What if Lena can be there but Marco can't?"*

> "Reschedule. I don't run kickoffs with three of four leads present. The fourth will need a re-run within a week regardless, and that's a coordination tax the engagement can't afford in week one."

**Q7 — Fallback when client wants to skip.** *"What if they say 'just start'?"*

> "I decline politely and explain the scope-change consequence. I offer a 90-minute compressed version if they're really squeezed for time. Skipping entirely is refused — that's the lesson from the three 2025 engagements that needed founder-led scope renegotiation, all three started with a thin or skipped kickoff."

**Q8 — Handoff after the workshop.** *"What happens in the next week?"*

> "Within 24 hours of the workshop, I write the engagement-scope document and circulate it to the four leads plus the client SPOC. I require written confirmation from all five within 48 hours. The first weekly check-in is scheduled at the workshop and lands within seven days — I run it, or if I'm unavailable, an account manager runs it with the engagement-scope document open in front of them."

**Q9 — Long-term ownership.** *"Once the engagement is moving, who owns the relationship?"*

> "From week 2 onwards, the discipline lead present at the workshop owns the relationship with their counterpart on the client side. I'm the orchestrator, not the work-doer. My job is to keep the four leads aligned with each other and with the SPOC; theirs is to do the work."

## Notes

- Tomás added at the end: "The kickoff is the most expensive thing we do operationally, and it's the cheapest thing we could do wrong. Get it right and the engagement runs itself; get it wrong and you spend the next 12 weeks paying for it."
